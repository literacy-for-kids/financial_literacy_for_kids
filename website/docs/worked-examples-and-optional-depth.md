# Worked Examples and Optional Depth

Use these cards after the corresponding core teaching. They supply the scenario and a sample response; no external research, new account, real-world contact, or private disclosure is required. The examples are fictional teaching cases, not real measurements, medical guidance, legal rulings, financial advice, or promises of outcomes.

## Two kinds of optional work

- **Supplied practice:** the scenario, sample response, and depth question are on this page. Allow about 15–20 minutes for one card, with paper or oral explanation. Read the card before the session; a calculator is optional for arithmetic.
- **Open research prompt:** a request to locate or construct missing teaching material, such as outside sources, interview records, or a real-case packet. This is not supplied instruction. A tool activity with complete steps is still supplied instruction, even when an adult must prepare the device or account. An adult must select and verify materials, arrange access, and plan additional time first. Use the supplied card instead when those materials are unavailable.

Choose depth by readiness and interest, not age alone. Learners aged 10–12 can use the core teaching without taking every technical add-on; older-learner work remains optional. The depth questions are not prerequisites for the next core week or criteria for its checkpoint.

## How to use a card

1. Read the scenario and ask for a reasoned answer.
2. Let the learner draw, speak, sort, or write using the fictional case.
3. Compare with the sample: notice the evidence and the limits, not matching wording.
4. If ready, change the stated assumption using the depth question.
5. If the core idea is unclear, return to the linked lesson before adding complexity. A private or paper artifact is enough.

Sample responses illustrate reasoning, not scripts to memorize or the only defensible answer to an open choice. They do not require a learner to experience or disclose the situation.

## Week 1
**Core lesson:** [Week 1: Understanding Value](./week01-understanding-value.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** A raincoat and a toy have the same price. Why might two people value them differently?

**Illustrative response:** A person caught in rain may value the coat more; another already has one. Value depends on needs and context, not only the price label.

**Optional depth:** Name a context change that could reverse the preference without ranking the people’s choices.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 2
**Core lesson:** [Week 2: Trade and Barter](./week02-trade-and-barter.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** Mina has extra pencils and wants paper; Sol has paper and wants pencils. What makes barter possible here?

**Illustrative response:** Each has something the other wants, and both can agree on an exchange. If Sol wanted a book instead, a direct trade might not work.

**Optional depth:** Add a third fictional trader with a book. Explain why the extra match makes barter harder to arrange.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 3
**Core lesson:** [Week 3: Why Money Exists](./week03-why-money-exists.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** A club accepts tokens for pencils and paper. What shared agreement makes the tokens useful?

**Illustrative response:** People expect others in the club to accept tokens later. The token helps compare prices and exchange goods without a direct barter match.

**Optional depth:** Explain why a token outside that agreement may not buy anything; do not infer that any invented token is real money.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 4
**Core lesson:** [Week 4: The Household Economy](./week04-the-household-economy.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** An invented household has $100 for a period. Required costs are $60, flexible plans $25, reserve $15. Does it balance?

**Illustrative response:** 60 + 25 + 15 = 100. If a required cost rises by $10, the plan needs a change; calling an expense flexible does not make every reduction easy.

**Optional depth:** Offer two revised fictional plans and describe their tradeoffs. Do not ask for actual family income.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 5
**Core lesson:** [Week 5: How Money Moves](./week05-how-money-moves.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** Rowan’s stub shows 10 hours at $12, $15 withholding and $5 benefit deduction. Explain the amount received.

**Illustrative response:** Gross pay is $120; deductions total $20; net pay is $100. A time record showing 12 hours is a reason to ask payroll to review the discrepancy.

**Optional depth:** At 12 hours gross becomes $144. Explain why a corrected net amount cannot be calculated without corrected deductions.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

**Supplied scenario and question:** A fictional account has $30. A $7 approved purchase posts. What should its ledger show?

**Illustrative response:** A $7 outflow leaves $23, assuming no other transactions or fees. The number represents a real balance in the example, even though no cash changed hands.

**Optional depth:** An unexpected message asks for a code to reverse a charge. Explain the verified adult/provider help route without sharing the code.

## Week 8
**Core lesson:** [Week 8: Friction and Spending](./week08-friction-and-spending.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** A game shop offers a one-click $4 add-on and a countdown. What makes a pause useful?

**Illustrative response:** Speed and urgency can reduce time to think. A pause lets the buyer check the full cost, purpose, and alternatives before choosing.

**Optional depth:** A fictional $4 monthly renewal costs $48 over 12 months before any other charges. Explain why one small price is not the whole cost.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 9
**Core lesson:** [Week 9: Opportunity Cost](./week09-opportunity-cost.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** A learner has $10 in pretend money. A book costs $10 and a kit costs $10. What is the opportunity cost of choosing the book?

**Illustrative response:** The next best available alternative given up is the kit. It is not automatically every possible thing money could buy.

**Optional depth:** Add a $6 option and explain how the leftover $4 changes the set of possible choices.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 10
**Core lesson:** [Week 10: Budgeting](./week10-budgeting.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** A pretend budget has $40 income, $18 spending, $12 saving, and $10 reserve. Check it.

**Illustrative response:** 18 + 12 + 10 = 40, so it allocates the available money. If income falls to $35, at least $5 must be reallocated.

**Optional depth:** Show two balanced $35 versions with different priorities; explain why arithmetic alone does not select the fairest plan.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 11
**Core lesson:** [Week 11: Risk and Emergency Funds](./week11-risk-and-emergency-funds.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** A fictional bike repair might cost $20. A plan sets aside $5 per period. What does four periods achieve?

**Illustrative response:** It builds a $20 reserve if the money stays saved. That can absorb the modeled repair; it does not cover every possible emergency.

**Optional depth:** Change the repair to $30 and identify the $10 gap. Do not imply that inability to save is a character failure.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 12
**Core lesson:** [Week 12: Banks](./week12-banks.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** A ledger begins at $50, receives $15, then pays $12. Explain the balance.

**Illustrative response:** 50 + 15 − 12 = $53, under the listed transactions. A bank/account record is a system for tracking money, not free extra money.

**Optional depth:** A displayed balance differs by $4. Identify records and pending transactions to check rather than inventing the cause.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 13
**Core lesson:** [Week 13: Interest](./week13-interest.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** In an invented simple-interest example, $100 earns $5 per year for two years. What happens with and without interest on prior interest?

**Illustrative response:** Simple interest gives $110. At a fictional 5% compounded yearly, year 1 is $105 and year 2 is $110.25. These are models, not promised returns.

**Optional depth:** If this were borrowing, explain that interest can be a cost; actual fees, timing, and terms matter.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 14
**Core lesson:** [Week 14: Inflation](./week14-inflation.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** A basket costs $10 in period 1 and $12 in period 2. How did its price change?

**Illustrative response:** It rose $2, or 20% of the starting $10. The same $10 buys less of that basket; one basket is not the whole economy’s inflation rate.

**Optional depth:** Explain why a $2 increase on a $20 basket is 10%, showing that the starting denominator matters.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 18
**Core lesson:** [Week 18: Sharing Value](./week18-sharing-value.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** Present a fictional tray plan that reduced missing pencils from 5 to 2 in one trial. What can you claim?

**Illustrative response:** "In this trial, 3 fewer pencils were missing using the same count. We should repeat it before claiming the tray always works."

**Optional depth:** Give one benefit, one cost, and one unresolved question; a public sales pitch or real purchase is not required.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

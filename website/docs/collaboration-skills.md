# Collaboration Skills for Money Choices

Financial literacy often includes shared resources, group goals, family conversations, classroom simulations, budgets, tradeoffs, and fairness. Collaboration helps kids talk about money choices respectfully — without shaming mistakes or putting adult responsibilities on children.

This page is the local doorway into the Literacy for Kids [Collaboration Toolkit](https://www.literacy-for-kids.com/docs/toolkits/collaboration), connected to the money skills this curriculum builds.

## A few core ideas

- **Shared money choices need clear goals.** "What are we trying to make possible?"
- **Fair does not always mean everyone gets the same thing.** Needs, access, and situations differ.
- **Tradeoffs can be discussed respectfully.** Disagreeing about wants is not an attack.
- **Kids should not be responsible for adult money problems.** Keep shared money choices kid-scale.

## When this shows up

- When a group has a pretend budget
- When a class project has limited resources
- When people disagree about wants and needs
- When saving for a shared goal
- When talking about fairness and tradeoffs

## Tools that help

- **Shared goal card** — what this money or resource is for.
- **Fair work / fair resource check** — does anyone have too much or too little?
- **Disagree and build frame** — concerns pointed at the tradeoff, not the person.
- **Check-in questions** — how is the shared goal going?
- **Trusted adult support** — for real money issues, which are adult jobs.

:::tip Collaboration Moment
A shared money choice needs a shared goal. Ask: "What are we trying to make possible with this money or resource?" That helps the group talk about tradeoffs instead of arguing about who gets their way.
:::

:::caution Everyday collaboration skills, not unsafe conflict management
These are everyday collaboration tools, not therapy, bullying intervention, conflict mediation, legal advice, or a replacement for trusted adults, educators, caregivers, or qualified support. Kids should not be expected to manage unsafe, coercive, bullying, discriminatory, or threatening group situations alone — and kids are not responsible for adult money problems. Keep examples kid-scale, classroom-scale, or fictional. If group work feels unsafe, targeted, or too big, involve a trusted adult.
:::
